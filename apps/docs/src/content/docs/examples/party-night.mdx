A small stage, a crowd, and an 85-second house set at 124 beats a minute. Every kick sends a ring
out from the booth, every clap throws stars from a speaker, the hats spark on the lighting rig, and
each drop fires two confetti cannons, fanned over 80° with `spread` and aimed in with `angle`.
Press play, with sound on.

## One score, two outputs

There is no audio file. The set is a list of events, such as a kick at 15.48 seconds or a clap at
15.97, and the same list feeds two things:

- **The synth.** The Web Audio API makes each event from oscillators and filtered noise a quarter
  of a second before it is due: sine kicks, noise claps and hats, a sawtooth bass, chord stabs and
  a riser. Seeking moves where that scheduling starts, so playback can begin from any point at once.
- **The show.** A paused GSAP timeline gets one motly burst per event, at the event's time. Specs
  are built from the event: the stab's color comes from its chord, and the build's roll grows
  step by step, with more Children, further out, as the drop nears.

So the bursts cannot drift from the music: they come from the same numbers.

## The audio clock owns time

The timeline never plays by itself. On each frame, it is moved to where the music is:

```js
gsap.ticker.add(() => {
  if (playing) show.time(context.currentTime - startedAt + offset - context.outputLatency);
});
```

Pause the music and the show stops; drag the slider and both jump together. (Rendering the whole
set into a buffer first would make seeking simpler, but it took about 12 seconds before anything
could play.) The audio's output
latency is taken off, so what you see matches what reaches the speakers, not what has only been
scheduled.

A burst under `@motly/gsap` is in the document only while its tween is between its ends, so a
timeline of about 700 bursts costs only the few that are playing. With `@motly/core`, a custom
Driver could follow the audio clock as well, but a core Timeline holds every Instance at its first
frame before it starts and draws it every frame. That suits a short sequence, not hundreds of
beats.

## Reduced motion and flashing

Bursts on every sixteenth note would flicker even as still frames. Under reduced motion the set
still plays, the stage colors still follow the chords, but the beat bursts and swinging beams are
left out: each section shows one still burst for its whole length instead.

At any setting, nothing flashes the whole stage. The kick rings, the largest thing on the beat,
come about twice a second, under the three flashes a second that WCAG 2.3.1 allows, and the faster
hat sparks are a few pixels across.
